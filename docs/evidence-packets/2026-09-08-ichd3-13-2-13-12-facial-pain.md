## 0. READ THIS FIRST — three findings that change the brief

### 0.1 NUMBERING DISCREPANCY (GPN) — the brief's "§13.2 Glossopharyngeal neuralgia" is not what the document says

The official ICHD-3 numbering is:

- **13.2** = *Pain attributed to a lesion or disease of the glossopharyngeal nerve* — a **parent heading with no diagnostic criteria of its own** (p. 171). Structurally identical to 13.1, which is *Pain attributed to a lesion or disease of the trigeminal nerve*.
- **13.2.1 Glossopharyngeal neuralgia** — this is the entity with criteria (p. 171).
- Subforms are **13.2.1.1 Classical / 13.2.1.2 Secondary / 13.2.1.3 Idiopathic** (all p. 172), **not** 13.2.1 / 13.2.2 / 13.2.3.
- **13.2.2 Painful glossopharyngeal neuropathy** (pp. 172–173) is a **sibling entity, not a subform of the neuralgia**, with its own sub-entries 13.2.2.1 and 13.2.2.2 (p. 173). It is the glossopharyngeal analogue of 13.1.2 Painful trigeminal neuropathy and is **out of scope**, on exactly the same reasoning the 2026-07-06 TN packet used to exclude 13.1.2.

**Consequence:** every claim ID, chip `teachWhenSelected`, registry `section` entry and displayed section label must read **13.2.1**, not 13.2. If the engine ships "ICHD-3 13.2" against these criteria it is citing a heading that contains no criteria.

### 0.2 NUMBERING CONFIRMED (PIFP) but criterion count is wrong in the brief

**13.12 Persistent idiopathic facial pain (PIFP)** is correct (p. 178). But the criteria run **A through F (six), not A–E**. The extra one is load-bearing: **criterion E is "A dental cause has been excluded by appropriate investigations"**, which is a workup gate, not a symptom.

Also correct one of the brief's stated negative features: PIFP has **no "unremarkable investigations" criterion**. The document says the opposite in the Comments (p. 179): *"psychophysical or neurophysiological tests may demonstrate sensory abnormalities."* The only investigation criterion is E (dental cause excluded); the only normal-findings criterion is D (clinical neurological examination is normal). Do not author "investigations unremarkable" as an ICHD-3 negative feature.

### 2.1 §13.2.1 Glossopharyngeal neuralgia (p. 171; Note and Comments p. 172)

**Previously used term (p. 171):** "Vagoglossopharyngeal neuralgia."

**Description (p. 171):** "A disorder characterized by unilateral brief stabbing pain, abrupt in onset and termination, in the distributions not only of the glossopharyngeal nerve but also of the auricular and pharyngeal branches of the vagus nerve. Pain is experienced in the ear, base of the tongue, tonsillar fossa and/or beneath the angle of the jaw. It is commonly provoked by swallowing, talking or coughing and may remit and relapse in the fashion of trigeminal neuralgia."

**Diagnostic criteria (p. 171):**

> **A.** Recurring paroxysmal attacks of unilateral pain in the distribution of the glossopharyngeal nerve¹ and fulfilling criterion B
> **B.** Pain has all of the following characteristics:
>  1. lasting from a few seconds to two minutes
>  2. severe intensity
>  3. electric shock-like, shooting, stabbing or sharp in quality
>  4. precipitated by swallowing, coughing, talking or yawning
> **C.** Not better accounted for by another ICHD-3 diagnosis.

**Note (p. 172):**

> 1. Within the posterior part of the tongue, tonsillar fossa, pharynx or angle of the lower jaw and/or in the ear.

**Comments (p. 172) — full, in printed order:**

> "13.2.1 *Glossopharyngeal neuralgia* can occur together with 13.1.1 *Trigeminal neuralgia*."
>
> "The superior laryngeal nerve is a branch of the vagus. Neuralgia of the superior laryngeal nerve presents similarly to 13.2.1 *Glossopharyngeal neuralgia* in its location and clinically can be difficult to distinguish from it."
>
> "Imaging may show neurovascular compression of the glossopharyngeal nerve."
>
> "Prior to development of 13.2.1 *Glossopharyngeal neuralgia*, unpleasant sensations may be felt in affected areas for weeks to several months."
>
> "The pain of 13.2.1 *Glossopharyngeal neuralgia* may radiate to involve the eye, nose, chin or shoulder. It can be severe enough for patients to lose weight. In rare cases, attacks of pain are associated with vagal symptoms such as cough, hoarseness, syncope and/or bradycardia. Some authors propose distinguishing between pharyngeal, otalgic and vagal subforms of neuralgia, and have suggested using the term *vagoglossopharyngeal neuralgia* when pain is accompanied by asystole, convulsions and syncope."
>
> "Clinical examination usually fails to show sensory changes in the nerve distribution but, if mild sensory deficits are encountered, they do not invalidate the diagnosis. Major changes or a reduced/missing gag reflex should prompt aetiological investigations."
>
> "13.2.1 *Glossopharyngeal neuralgia* is usually responsive, at least initially, to pharmacotherapy (especially carbamazepine or oxcarbazepine). It has been suggested that application of local anaesthetic to the tonsil and pharyngeal wall can prevent attacks for a few hours."

### 2.2 §13.2.1.1 Classical glossopharyngeal neuralgia (p. 172)

**Description (p. 172):** "Glossopharyngeal neuralgia developing without apparent cause other than neurovascular compression."

**Diagnostic criteria (p. 172):**

> **A.** Recurrent paroxysms of unilateral pain fulfilling criteria for 13.2.1 *Glossopharyngeal neuralgia*
> **B.** Demonstration on MRI or during surgery of neurovascular compression of the glossopharyngeal nerve root.

No Notes. No Comments.

**Encoding trap:** unlike **13.1.1.1 Classical TN**, whose criterion B requires "neurovascular compression (**not simply contact**), **with morphological changes** in the trigeminal nerve root" (p. 167), **13.2.1.1 criterion B has no morphological-change qualifier and no not-simply-contact qualifier**. Do not copy the TN chip semantics across. The classical/idiopathic split for GPN turns on whether compression is demonstrated at all, not on whether it caused atrophy or displacement.

---

### 2.3 §13.2.1.2 Secondary glossopharyngeal neuralgia (p. 172)

**Description (p. 172):** "Glossopharyngeal neuralgia caused by an underlying disease."

**Diagnostic criteria (p. 172):**

> **A.** Recurrent paroxysms of unilateral pain fulfilling criteria for 13.2.1 *Glossopharyngeal neuralgia*
> **B.** An underlying disease has been demonstrated known to be able to cause, and explaining, the neuralgia.¹

**Note (p. 172):**

> 1. There are single reports of 13.2.1.2 *Secondary glossopharyngeal neuralgia* caused by neck trauma, multiple sclerosis, tonsillar or regional tumours, cerebellopontine angle tumours and Arnold–Chiari malformation.

No Comments. **Note the absence of a criterion C** ("not better accounted for by another ICHD-3 diagnosis") — 13.2.1.2 has only A and B, whereas its TN counterpart 13.1.1.2 has A, B and C. This is the document as printed; do not add a phantom criterion C.

---

### 2.4 §13.2.1.3 Idiopathic glossopharyngeal neuralgia (p. 172)

**Description (p. 172):** "Glossopharyngeal neuralgia with no evidence either of neurovascular compression or of causative underlying disease."

**Diagnostic criteria (p. 172):**

> **A.** Recurrent paroxysms of unilateral pain fulfilling criteria for 13.2.1 *Glossopharyngeal neuralgia*
> **B.** Investigations have found neither neurovascular compression nor an underlying disease known to be able to cause 13.2.1.2 *Secondary glossopharyngeal neuralgia*
> **C.** Not better accounted for by another ICHD-3 diagnosis.

No Notes. No Comments. **No equivalent of the TN 13.1.1.3 Note 1** (the "contact without morphological change is still idiopathic" rule). That rule is TN-only and must not be transplanted.

### 2.5 Scope boundary — §13.2.2 Painful glossopharyngeal neuropathy (pp. 172–173) — OUT OF SCOPE

Included verbatim only because it is the mutual-exclusion partner and the writer must not import it into the neuralgia phenotype.

**Description (p. 172):** "Pain within the distribution of the glossopharyngeal nerve (posterior part of the tongue, tonsillar fossa, pharynx and/or beneath the angle of the lower jaw). In addition, pain is commonly perceived in the ipsilateral ear. The primary pain is usually continuous or near-continuous, and commonly described as burning or squeezing, or likened to pins and needles. Brief paroxysms may be superimposed, but they are not the predominant pain type. This combination distinguishes painful glossopharyngeal neuropathy from the subforms of 13.2.1 *Glossopharyngeal neuralgia*. Sensory deficits may be present in the ipsilateral posterior part of the tongue and tonsillar fossa, and the gag reflex may be weak or missing."

Sub-entries **13.2.2.1 Painful glossopharyngeal neuropathy attributed to a known cause** and **13.2.2.2 Idiopathic painful glossopharyngeal neuropathy** are both on p. 173. Not transcribed here; explicitly excluded from this Class E scope, exactly as 13.1.2 was excluded from the 2026-07-06 TN packet.

## 3. Verbatim — §13.12 Persistent idiopathic facial pain

### 3.1 §13.12 Persistent idiopathic facial pain (PIFP) (p. 178; Comments p. 179)

**Previously used term (p. 178):** "Atypical facial pain."

**Description (p. 178):** "Persistent facial and/or oral pain, with varying presentations but recurring daily for more than two hours/day over more than three months, in the absence of clinical neurological deficit."

**Diagnostic criteria (p. 178):**

> **A.** Facial and/or oral pain fulfilling criteria B and C
> **B.** Recurring daily for >2 hours/day for >3 months
> **C.** Pain has both of the following characteristics:
>  1. poorly localized, and not following the distribution of a peripheral nerve
>  2. dull, aching or nagging quality
> **D.** Clinical neurological examination is normal
> **E.** A dental cause has been excluded by appropriate investigations
> **F.** Not better accounted for by another ICHD-3 diagnosis.

**Notes:** **none printed.** §13.12 carries no Note block and no superscript note markers on any criterion. This is a genuine absence, verified by reading the full printed entry across pp. 178–179 in reading order, not an extraction gap.

**Comments (p. 179) — full, in printed order:**

> "A wide variety of words are used by patients to describe the character of 13.12 *Persistent idiopathic facial pain* but it is most often depicted as dull, nagging or aching, either deep or superficial. It can have sharp exacerbations, and is aggravated by stress. Over time, it may spread to a wider area of the craniocervical region."
>
> "Patients with 13.12 *Persistent idiopathic facial pain* are predominantly female."
>
> "13.12 *Persistent idiopathic facial pain* may be comorbid with other pain conditions such as chronic widespread pain and irritable bowel syndrome. In addition, it presents with high levels of psychiatric comorbidity and psychosocial disability."
>
> "13.12 *Persistent idiopathic facial pain* may originate from a minor operation or injury to the face, maxillae, teeth or gums, but persists after healing of the initial noxious event without any demonstrable local cause. However, psychophysical or neurophysiological tests may demonstrate sensory abnormalities. A continuum seems to exist from 13.12 *Persistent idiopathic facial pain* induced by insignificant trauma to 13.1.2.3 *Painful post-traumatic trigeminal neuropathy* caused obviously by significant insult to the peripheral nerves."
>
> "The term *atypical odontalgia* has been applied to a continuous pain in one or more teeth or in a tooth socket after extraction, in the absence of any usual dental cause. This is thought to be a subtype of 13.12 *Persistent idiopathic facial pain* although it is more localized, the mean age at onset is younger and genders are more balanced. Based on the history of trauma, atypical odontalgia may also be a subform of 13.1.2.3 *Painful post-traumatic trigeminal neuropathy*. These subtypes/forms, if they exist, have not been sufficiently studied to propose diagnostic criteria."

**No subforms.** §13.12 has no numbered sub-entries. The final Comment is explicit that the candidate subtypes (atypical odontalgia) are not sufficiently studied to propose criteria. **The engine must not invent an atypical-odontalgia subform.**

### 3.2 Adjacent-entity boundary — §13.11 Burning mouth syndrome (p. 178) — needed for the differential, out of scope for encoding

PIFP's nearest neighbour shares criterion B **word for word**, so the writer needs this text to build the discriminator honestly.

**Description (p. 178):** "An intraoral burning or dysaesthetic sensation, recurring daily for more than two hours/day over more than three months, without clinically evident causative lesions."

**Diagnostic criteria (p. 178):**

> **A.** Oral pain¹ fulfilling criteria B and C
> **B.** Recurring daily for >2 hours/day for >3 months
> **C.** Pain has both of the following characteristics:
>  1. burning quality²
>  2. felt superficially in the oral mucosa
> **D.** Oral mucosa is of normal appearance and clinical examination including sensory testing is normal
> **E.** Not better accounted for by another ICHD-3 diagnosis.

**Notes (p. 178):**
> 1. The pain is usually bilateral; the most common site is the tip of the tongue.
> 2. Pain intensity fluctuates.

**Discriminator in one line, sourced:** identical B; PIFP C.1 is "poorly localized, and not following the distribution of a peripheral nerve" and C.2 "dull, aching or nagging" versus BMS C.1 "burning quality" and C.2 "felt superficially in the oral mucosa". Quality plus depth is the entire separation.

### 4.1 Glossopharyngeal neuralgia — what the article actually says (pp. 479–480)

The GPN section runs from the bottom of p. 479 to the top-middle of p. 480. **Verbatim, complete:**

> **"GLOSSOPHARYNGEAL NEURALGIA** (p. 479) Glossopharyngeal neuralgia is very similar to trigeminal neuralgia in many respects, except for the nerve involved and the presence of some unique symptoms. Overlap with trigeminal neuralgia symptomatology can exist and the *(p. 480)* two can occur simultaneously in the same individual. Pain is not necessarily restricted to the distribution of the nerve and may be perceived in the eye, nose, chin, larynx, or shoulder. Due to proximity and interconnectedness with other structures, particularly the vagus nerve, symptoms such as cough, hoarseness, bradycardia, and syncope can be present. As with trigeminal neuralgia, glossopharyngeal neuralgia is divided into classical, secondary, and idiopathic etiologies, and may similarly present with purely paroxysmal or continuous pain, or as a neuropathic pain syndrome. **Treatment overlaps substantially with trigeminal neuralgia.** Like trigeminal neuralgia, glossopharyngeal neuralgia is rare, but the incidence and prevalence are uncertain. The diagnostic criteria are listed in TABLE 10-3."

**Key Point sidebar (p. 477):**
> "Glossopharyngeal neuralgia shares many similarities with trigeminal neuralgia, but the pain often involves areas outside the distribution of the nerve, and other nonpain symptoms may be present."

**How this differs from what is currently in the registry.** "Airway protection mandatory if swallowing affected" asserts an aspiration-risk mechanism and issues a mandatory instruction. The sources describe a **vagally-mediated cardiac/syncopal** mechanism (bradycardia, asystole, syncope, convulsions) and a **nutritional** consequence (weight loss), and they issue exactly one investigative directive (reduced or missing gag reflex prompts aetiological investigation). Those are different clinical claims. The registry text should be replaced, not softened.

**Escalation, per my scope boundary:** whether a syncope/asystole presentation warrants a cardiac-monitoring or admission steer in the NeuroWiki pathway is a clinical-recommendation judgement. That is `medical-scientist`'s call with `clinical-reviewer` gating, not mine. I have supplied the sourced substrate; I am not adjudicating the recommendation.

1. **Do not clone the TN chip set for GPN.** GPN's trigger is a *specific mechanical/deglutitive* set (swallowing, coughing, talking, yawning), not TN's *innocuous cutaneous stimuli*.
